The last three weeks I spent in the south of France were calm and pleasant, yet monotone to say the least. I remember my first impressions of the South of France when I had visited Marseilles three or four years ago and that really shocked me. It was such a bustling, immensely beautiful city and it had shattered the notions that I had of such a Western European country like France. In my mind, countries like France, Belgium, the Netherlands, also Sweden had been places that were the polar opposite to the chaos I was used to in India. Clean, structured streets with picturesquely designed and placed buildings as opposed to the sheer havoc that was each road and neighborhood in Chennai, the smell of earth and smoke and mullapoo (jasmine) garlands and the constant drill of autorickshaws or a lorry driver's proud announcement of his presence through his lorry's melodious, deafening horn, as opposed to quaint, quiet streets where raising your voice would garner people's stares. And I say this with all my love for India and how it feels like home, but the difference could not be more stark. But Marseilles had weirdly reminded me of India. It was loud, dirty, stunning and so communal. The latter and the resemblance to India I had felt before when I had visited Spain, but to me, Spain was different fundamentally to its Western counterparts. And then I went to Nice.

Nice is so weird, like honestly. As you drive outside of the airport, along the beach and the ocean, and you see all the runners jogging along the coast, glancing at their watches, families drying themselves after a visit to the beach with their strollers bearing laughing babies, and all these pretty European people strolling along, satisfaction in their faces and freckles on their cheeks. And oh my days, so tanned. I had never thought that white people could be this brown, hell, it reminded me funnily of home because I was finally surrounded by people the same colour as me. The sand and the ocean are Instagram-perfect, the roads and walkways so clean, benches everywhere and pretty little nooks of shade in front of neatly manicured bushes of rosemary and oleander. Perfect people walking their perfect dogs in their perfect outfits adorning their perfect bodies. I had described it then as feeling, frankly, simulated. Like something engineered by the game developers of the Sims or GTA. It felt impossible that people actually lived here. Kind of like those IKEA showrooms that the workers try so hard to seem 'lived in' by throwing a spare pillow in a spot that just happens to the most aesthetically pleasing possible position, or those books that lay perfectly scattered across a table or a child's drawing that hangs crooked but at precisely the choicest angle, and no matter these attempts, something about these rooms feel so off, so artificial.  And my saying this didn't come from a place of envy or contempt or anything else, just pure curiousity that the people that live here have the same worries and feelings and stories that I do. That whatever their imperfections are don't seem to translate into this idyllic lifestyle that they live. It's different.

And this sentiment of artificiality is probably because people actually don't live here. And fair enough, Nice has been a holiday hotspot for the rich and famous ever since after World War II, if not before. Not to mention a huge tourist destination. It's when you get to the areas outside of Nice where things seem a little different. In the inside of Cagnes-Sur-Mer, for example, the streets smell like piss and you have to watch your step to not accidentally walk on dog shit. The small street supermarkets or sandwich delis don't look as polished or aesthetic as they do closer to the city. This is, I guess, the more impoverished or 'ghetto' area of the Riviera where immigrants or locals that work in the city choose to live, probably due to the high prices of the city. The quality of living is obviously still much higher than the median one in India, who are we kidding, and I guess these kind of areas are common to all major touristy cities. And I think the word I would use to describe the people of Cagnes-Sur-Mer is relaxed. So, so relaxed. As I go on my run I pass by the same group of men sitting together for at least an hour and chatting while having a coffee and a cigarette at always the same café, or a group of French teenagers zooming around aimlessly on their Lime bikes, or the same couple that always go on a run together at the same time everyday, and chat and point at things as they run. It's really different to the city life I'm used to in Stockholm or in the Netherlands, where everyone follows their schedules on their Google calendar, and only frequent a café or any other space because of a pre-planned hangout. I definitely waqnt to experience that kind of life, where I can just go to a spot and just expect to find people there, and we can chat for neverending hours. This kind of thing is very southern European I think. 

And not to say that Nice is solely an ultra-curated playground for the rich. I was reading a book on a bench in a park in the centre of Nice, and a couple in their fifties came to sit next to me. They chatted with me for a bit with their broken English and after conversation got a bit too difficult logistically, I went back to my book. I learned that the lady was not originally from Nice but had lived here for the past twenty years after falling in love with the city. As I read my book, I also saw them strike up conversations with passerbys, compliment people's dogs, chat with a cleaner who walked by with a large trolley. I had thought that maybe these people knew eachother, but then my broken French comprehension caught the lady's words 'Vous habitez ici?' to one of these people, and that surprised me. The fact that they were just casually chatting with people they did not know before, on an ordinary evening stroll, at a park they probably visited everyday and registered all other visiters, was something that my Swedish-turned brain could not comprehend. Even in India, in the cities, this is becoming increasingly abnormal because people are so, so busy, and they grow increasingly isolated by being focused solely on their careers, friends, families etc. This was very refreshing.

I'd say maybe warmer weather opens people up, but then we have India, but even India is a special case where people pride on being more Western. Just a stupid, arbitrary thing, but I think the common choice of footwear among people doesn't just reflect the climate of the place they live, but also something about the current values. In Sweden and the Netherlands, you obviously have sneakers or boots, which makes sense logistically, and also reflects how people are, not unfriendly, but definitely a bit more closed off and less approachable or approaching. In the south of France, everyone wore flip flops, definitely because of the heat, but also signifying just how relaxed and casual the atmosphere here was, both in Nice and Cagnes-Sur-Mer. This used to be the case in India as well, at least up until I left in 2018, such that now when I go back, people are increasingly wearing sneakers or closed toe shoes, which in my mind makes no sense to wear outside of a more formal setting. Maybe sun protection or something, I'm not sure, but it's a small thing that to me shows how European urban India is trying to become, or becoming. Perhaps this relaxed thing works for Nice because more people here are retired, or live a quality of life such that working is not their top priority, and this is obviously not the case for most of India. But it's a bit sad that we're losing more of the communal-ness of India that we always prided ourselved on, and I feel really sad about how Swedish and closed-off I've become, how I have less energy for conversations and spending time with other people. In any case, I think the south of France has become more multi-faceted in my mind than it was judging from my first impressions of Nice. And I kind of want to take inspiration from the people here, to just relax a bit more, and not set such high standards for myself because I've been blessed with a great life that the majority of India doesn't have. At least I know they're taking inspiration from me through my skin colour lol.
